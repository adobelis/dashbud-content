# Who Captures the Value in AI?

### From chips to applications: mapping the AI stack and where the value might accrue.

Jo Clodic Jul 07, 2026

Every technological revolution produces an ecosystem: infrastructure providers, toolmakers, and application builders. The technology creates value across all of them, but it does not distribute it evenly, and the layer that creates the most value is rarely the one that captures it.

The internet created more value at the application layer (Google, Amazon, Facebook) than at the infrastructure layer (Cisco, telecoms). The smartphone era produced more value at the platform layer (Apple, Google) than among the handset manufacturers.

For investors, the central question is not whether AI will be transformative, but where in the stack the value will actually accrue.

To think clearly about value capture, you first need to understand the stack.

## The AI Stack

### Infrastructure

Infrastructure is the physical foundation: the chips, the factories, and the cloud capacity that runs everything above it.

Behind every AI model sits a tightly concentrated supply chain:

- ASML makes the **lithography machines** that can print the sub-nanometre circuit patterns that modern chips require — a monopoly so complete that every advanced chip manufacturer in the world depends on it.

- NVIDIA designs the **GPUs** that have become the dominant compute unit for AI training and inference; it currently controls roughly 85% of the GPU market.

- SK Hynix, Samsung, and Micron dominate **high-bandwidth memory** (HBM) — the specialised chips that feed data to GPUs fast enough to keep them running at capacity.

- TSMC is the pure-play foundry that handles **chip manufacturing** at scale. It operates fabs that cost tens of billions of dollars to build and take years to bring online.
- Above chip design and manufacturing sit the **cloud providers** — AWS, Azure, Google Cloud, and specialist GPU clouds like CoreWeave — that rent compute capacity to anyone who needs it without buying hardware outright.

##### Economics

The physical layer (TSMC, ASML) is capital-intensive, highly concentrated, and has long investment cycles. Its economics resemble utilities more than software: enormous fixed costs, long asset lives, and pricing power that derives from physical scarcity rather than network effects.

At the chip design layer (NVIDIA), ecosystem is a key competitive advantage: CUDA, the programming framework that runs on NVIDIA chips, has two decades of developer tooling, libraries, and institutional knowledge built around it. Switching away from NVIDIA means rewriting software stacks, not just swapping hardware. This is why NVIDIA has sustained margins closer to a platform company than a hardware manufacturer.

Cloud providers benefited from GPU scarcity during the supply crunch of 2023–2024: those who secured early allocations could charge a premium. But unlike chip manufacturing, where scarcity is more structural and pricing power more entrenched, cloud GPU scarcity is cyclical: as supply scales, competition on price, reliability, and ecosystem increasingly determines who wins.

The hyperscalers (AWS, Azure, GCP) have the advantage of bundling GPU access with broader cloud services; specialist providers like CoreWeave compete on raw GPU availability and pricing, with a thinner moat.

### Models

A small number of organisations are training frontier models at scale: OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, Meta (whose LLaMA series is open-source), and xAI. The economics are more reminiscent of traditional, capital-intensive businesses rather than software: high upfront training costs, falling inference costs as efficiency improves. A single training run can cost hundreds of millions of dollars, creating high barriers to entry and, for now, a defensible position for incumbents. Open-source models create a constant ceiling on what closed-source frontier models can charge, reinforcing the competitive dynamic.

Feeding into this layer are data inputs. Pre-training draws on web-scale corpora (CommonCrawl), human-labelled datasets (Surge AI), and specialised human data providers (Karya, Mercor). The quality of a model is only as good as the quality of its training data, and that pipeline still requires significant human input. Post-training — the fine-tuning and reinforcement learning from human feedback that shapes model behaviour — relies on data infrastructure platforms like Databricks and Snowflake, and increasingly on synthetic data generated by the models themselves.

### Orchestration and tooling

Tools connect models to applications. They include orchestration frameworks (LangChain), vector databases that give models access to external knowledge (Pinecone), model monitoring and evaluation tools (Weights & Biases), and model repositories (Hugging Face). This layer is more fragmented and its competitive landscape more fluid than the model layer: categories that were distinct products in 2023 are being absorbed into foundation model APIs or consolidated into broader platforms. Parts of this layer are already commoditising; others (agent runtimes, evaluation infrastructure) remain unsettled.

At the boundary with applications sit a new category of AI-native software engineering tools: IDEs (Cursor, Windsurf), coding agents (Claude Code, OpenAI Codex), app builders (Lovable), and autonomous SWE agents (Cognition, Factory) — tools that are beginning to automate the work of writing, building, and reviewing code end-to-end, blurring the line between developer tooling and finished product.

### Applications

##### Enterprise

Applications are where AI capability meets end users and business workflows. This layer is the most diverse and the most competitive. It subdivides into three broad categories.

*Horizontal tools* serve knowledge workers across industries: enterprise search (Glean), customer service agents (Sierra), meeting intelligence, writing assistants. The addressable market is enormous — every knowledge worker is a potential user — but competition is intense and switching costs are low.

*Vertical AI* builds AI-native software for specific industries: legal (Harvey), clinical documentation (Abridge), financial services (Hebbia), construction (Procore). Vertical applications combine deep workflow integration with purpose-built interfaces designed around how professionals in that field actually work. Moats here rest on proprietary data, workflow depth, and regulatory requirements that general-purpose tools cannot easily replicate.

Cutting across both categories is the emergence of agents: autonomous systems that complete entire workflows rather than assisting with individual tasks.

The business model of the application layer is evolving. With traditional SaaS, the cost of developing software was fixed, while the marginal cost of serving an additional user was near zero. With AI applications, by contrast, every interaction triggers inference costs, making flat-rate seat licensing difficult to sustain at scale.

Two new models are emerging: usage-based pricing (charging per query, document, or API call), which aligns better with AI’s variable cost structure; and outcome-based pricing (charging per resolved ticket or completed transaction), which aligns with the value delivered. Longer term, as agents mature and begin replacing rather than assisting workers, the case for seat-based pricing weakens further still.

##### Consumer

*Consumer AI* includes ChatGPT, Perplexity, and Character.ai, alongside creative tools like Midjourney and Suno that have opened entirely new market categories. Economics differ from enterprise: faster growth, higher churn, subscription or advertising monetisation, viral distribution. So far, most entrepreneurial energy in generative AI has concentrated on B2B, but consumer applications may be where the most surprising new categories emerge, as the technology matures and developers move beyond productivity into entertainment, creativity, and companionship.

## Where Does the Value Go?

The harder question for investors is not what the stack looks like, but who makes money along the way.

The historical argument, based on recent technological waves, is that infrastructure commoditises and applications capture durable value. Utilities did not capture most of the value created by electricity; factories reorganising their operations around it did. Telecoms did not capture most of the value created by the internet; Google, Amazon, Facebook and other software providers did. AWS did not capture most of the economic value created by cloud computing; Salesforce, Shopify, Stripe, and hundreds of vertical SaaS companies did. The analogy would suggest that NVIDIA and the hyperscalers will be competed down over time, model APIs will become cheap inputs like electricity, and application companies will capture most of the value. But this is not an iron law, and AI might well break this pattern.

Three broad views compete on where AI value will accrue.

- **Models commoditise, applications win:** as the enabling layer gets competed down, value accrues to whoever builds most effectively on top of it.
- **Models eat applications**: improving foundation models perform the core function of AI applications natively, commoditising the layer above faster than moats can be built.
- **It depends which applications.**Those embedded in high-stakes, data-intensive workflows and regulated industries accumulate moats naturally; those that merely repackage general-purpose model outputs (so-called wrappers) do not.

The best way to evaluate these competing narratives is to work through the stack layer by layer.

### Infrastructure

##### Bull: picks and shovels win

The picks-and-shovels argument is compelling: NVIDIA wins whether OpenAI or Anthropic dominates at the model layer; the hyperscalers win regardless of which applications succeed.

AI adoption is still early: as industries from healthcare to manufacturing to logistics integrate AI into core workflows, inference demand grows with every new deployment. Industry analysts estimate that inference will represent 70-80% of compute spending in the coming years, with Jensen Huang declaring that “the inference inflection has arrived”.

NVIDIA’s moat runs deeper than market share. The CUDA ecosystem creates switching costs that go well beyond hardware: the global ML research community has been built on it, and no alternative has matched its depth of tooling and institutional adoption. Alternatives (AMD’s ROCm, custom silicon from Google and Amazon) are closing the gap slowly, but the switching cost remains substantial.

Beyond chips, there are still significant technical problems to solve at the infrastructure layer: cooling, power delivery, high-speed interconnects, advanced packaging. Each represents its own value creation opportunity for the companies that crack them.

For investors, the picks-and-shovels thesis has one benefit: it does not require predicting who wins the AI race downstream of infrastructure: which model provider, which application, or even which layer between the two. It only requires that AI adoption continues.

##### Bear: demand risk and architecture shifts

Infrastructure economics are not software economics: capital intensity is extreme, asset lives are long, and the bottleneck shifts.

The bull case depends on AI demand continuing to grow, which ultimately rests on AI delivering measurable value in applications. If productivity gains prove smaller or slower than expected, enterprise budgets tighten, and the entire infrastructure buildout is exposed as overbuilt relative to actual demand. Hyperscalers committed roughly $750 billion to AI infrastructure between 2024 and 2025; if the applications layer fails to generate sufficient returns to justify that spending, the asset base could be significantly impaired.

The second risk is architecture. The current infrastructure buildout is optimised for transformer-based models trained on text and code at scale. Several prominent researchers, such as Yann LeCun, argue that transformers have fundamental limitations for physical-world reasoning and true general intelligence, and that the next paradigm will require world models or other architectures with very different compute profiles. If they are right, the infrastructure stack optimised for today’s models may not be the one that powers tomorrow’s, and the companies that have built around current architectures face stranded asset risk.

Infrastructure has historically earned large revenues but not extraordinary profits: the companies that built the internet’s physical backbone did not capture most of its value. ASML, NVIDIA, and the hyperscalers are so far defying that pattern; the question is whether today’s extraordinary margins reflect a temporary scarcity premium or a durable structural advantage.

### Models

##### Bear case: models get commoditised

The model layer faces structural pressure from two directions.

The first is the competitive dynamic between the frontier labs themselves: OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google take turns at the capability frontier every few weeks, but no one holds a durable lead; and because switching costs between them are low, CIOs are beginning to treat them as interchangeable. This is a recipe for price competition, not pricing power.

The second is the proliferation of cheaper alternatives: open-source models, smaller variants (4o Mini, Gemini Flash, Claude Haiku). These deliver near-frontier performance at a fraction of the cost, and for most enterprise tasks, they are already good enough. Enterprises are already deploying “advisor model” architectures: a router hands the bulk of work to a cheap model, escalating to a frontier one only for the most demanding tasks. As capability compounds, even older frontier models become sufficient for the vast majority of use cases, leaving premium pricing confined to an ever-narrowing set of genuinely hard problems.

Together, these forces could place a ceiling on what proprietary model providers can charge.

The trajectory of the model layer also depends critically on whether scaling — more compute, more data, larger models — continues to drive capability improvements. If it plateaus, efficiency and architectural innovation matter more than raw compute, open-source closes the gap further, and the frontier premium erodes faster than anyone currently expects.

##### Bull case: scaling continues, frontier tokens command a premium

The bull case is that frontier tokens will always command a premium. Complex reasoning, novel scientific problems, and high-stakes enterprise decisions require the best available model; and the gap between frontier and open-source, while narrowing, has repeatedly reopened as the labs push further ahead. Token consumption of frontier models remains massive and growing, despite the inroads open-source models have made in relative share:

The barriers to training frontier models are not just financial but organisational: the research talent, accumulated know-how, and proprietary training infrastructure at OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google DeepMind are not easily replicated. If scaling continues (and so far it has continued despite repeated predictions of plateau), frontier labs extend their lead and their pricing power with it.

The frontier labs also enjoy structural advantages beyond inference pricing: distribution at scale (ChatGPT at over a billion monthly users), the ability to observe usage patterns across millions of enterprise deployments, and the research talent to build application-layer products faster than most startups. The most valuable model companies may ultimately be valued less like API providers and more like vertically integrated platforms.

### Tooling and orchestration

This layer will produce valuable businesses: model repositories, evaluation infrastructure, and monitoring tools are genuinely necessary, and some will achieve durable positions. But it is also the most unstable: categories commoditise quickly as foundation model APIs absorb adjacent functionality, and the line between tooling and model capability shifts constantly. The bulk of the value capture debate plays out elsewhere: in infrastructure, at the model layer, and in applications.

### Applications

##### Horizontal applications

**Bull case:**Horizontal applications have the largest addressable market. Every knowledge worker across every industry is a potential user. The companies that establish early distribution and accumulate usage data at scale can build network effects and data advantages before vertical entrants claim individual categories. In this environment, speed of execution is key. Some horizontal products sit close enough to core workflows to accumulate meaningful switching costs over time.

The **bear case** is the wrapper problem: tools that do one thing well for knowledge workers — without owning the data layer or embedding in mission-critical workflows — have no defensibility against a foundation model that ships the same capability natively tomorrow.

The frontier labs are already moving downstream into horizontal categories: OpenAI’s Frontier platform deploys agents across enterprise workflows; Google’s Gemini for Workspace competes directly with horizontal productivity and knowledge management tools. As foundation models improve, they increasingly perform the core function of horizontal applications natively, commoditising the layer faster than moats can be built.

The companies most at risk are those whose core function is a capability the underlying model is improving toward, and whose customer relationship is shallow enough that switching is painless. Sam Altman captured this in a heuristic for founders: if the next model release strengthens your product, you are probably building something defensible; if it risks replacing it, you are probably a wrapper.

##### Vertical applications

Vertical applications are where the strongest application-layer moats are being built.

The **bull case** rests on five compounding advantages:

- Proprietary data: Abridge has processed millions of medical encounters; Harvey has indexed legal documents across dozens of countries. Each new customer interaction makes the product better, and that dataset cannot be replicated by a new entrant starting from zero.
- Workflow depth: the most defensible products span many steps, each touching different data sources and requiring domain-specific logic. They also develop purpose-built interfaces designed around how professionals in that field actually work, which takes significant domain knowledge and iteration time to develop.
- Customer proximity: companies embedded in mission-critical workflows accumulate direct exposure to edge cases, informal workarounds, and operational exceptions that determine whether an AI system actually works in practice — and in regulated industries, trust built over years of relationships that a new entrant cannot buy quickly.
- Switching costs: once a system is embedded in core operations, the financial and organisational costs of replacing it are high. Custom workflows that take six to twelve months to pilot mean that once a company is wired into operations, re-running a competitive bake-off is seen as a waste of time.
- Regulatory moat: in healthcare, legal, and financial services, compliance requirements and audit trails demand deep domain investment upfront that compounds into a structural barrier general-purpose entrants cannot shortcut.

The most durable companies combine defensive moats — regulatory certification, compliance infrastructure — that slow down competitors, with generative moats — compounding data, expanding workflow coverage — that actively widen the gap over time.

The **bear case** is that the same frontier labs moving into horizontal applications have even stronger incentives to enter the highest-margin verticals directly. Anthropic has built enterprise agent workflows targeting finance, legal, and engineering. The bespoke vertical model argument cuts both ways: if fine-tuned domain models outperform general frontier models, nothing stops the labs from building those models themselves, with more resources than any startup. The defensibility case depends on proprietary data compounding before model improvement makes that advantage irrelevant. And the window for building moats may be narrowing: enterprises are moving from pilot to production faster than expected, giving vertical AI companies less time to become the chosen system of record.

## Conclusion

Whether infrastructure commoditises as it did in previous waves remains uncertain. Railway tracks, telephone networks, and electricity grids were eventually standardised and competed down; but AI infrastructure is technologically more complex and faster-moving. The cutting edge of chip design, memory architecture, and data centre efficiency is itself a moving target, which makes replication harder. Nor are chips the only constraint: land, power, grid connection, and planning permission for data centres are increasingly emerging as the key bottlenecks at the infrastructure layer.

Within applications, the evidence so far points toward bifurcation, and the market is already pricing it in. Public SaaS multiples compressed sharply in early 2026, triggered in part by the launch of autonomous AI agents capable of replacing entire workflow categories. Individual stocks like HubSpot fell 39% and the SaaS Capital Index dropped to 3.8x ARR by March 2026, its lowest level in over a decade. But the sell-off was not uniform. Software multiples in June 2026 show clear segmentation: vertical AI-native applications trade at strong levels, while horizontal SaaS shows wide valuation dispersion — a sign of AI’s uneven impact on the application layer.

Ultimately, value capture is time-dependent: pricing power follows the current bottleneck, which shifts as the tech stack matures — both within and between layers. Within infrastructure, the bottleneck has already shifted from GPUs to high-bandwidth memory, power, and data centre capacity. Across the stack, today’s bottleneck is infrastructure; tomorrow it may be frontier intelligence; later, it may be proprietary data, workflow integration, or customer relationships.

The right frame is therefore not “infrastructure vs. applications” but: where is the bottleneck today, which companies are anticipating it, and which ones are building moats fast enough to matter when it moves?
