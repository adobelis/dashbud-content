# What We Told Our LPs About AI — Jesse Middleton, Flybridge

## Summary

Flybridge's 2026 LP meeting letter. Main argument: rather than waiting for next-generation models, there is roughly 100x more value to unlock by building applications on the models that exist today. The gap between how power users work and how everyone else still works is the opportunity.

Four evidence gaps:
1. **User gap.** Fewer than 10M heavy AI users support ~$175B in annualized AI revenue, against 1.5B knowledge workers.
2. **Enterprise gap.** Fewer than 1 in 10 enterprises have agents in production, while (per Anthropic's labor market study) current models could automate ~90% of office and admin roles.
3. **Small business gap.** ~75% of small businesses use AI; almost none run daily operations on it. Portfolio example Topline Pro: 4+ agents per service provider, $655M in bookings.
4. **Internal evidence.** Flybridge OS token usage up 23x in six months, growing 70% month over month, on a 10-person team.

Four investment areas:
1. **The agent economy.** Agents as customers, coworkers, and counterparties of each other. Portfolio: Arcade (identity and permissions for agents), Limy (agent-to-enterprise distribution).
2. **Models beyond the frontier labs.** Open-weight, small, deterministic, and scientific models for specialized needs. Portfolio: Arcee. Distinguishes closed-weight, open-weight, open-source.
3. **Unpromptable markets.** Vertical industries the frontier labs won't invest in because the markets are small, but where venture returns are large. Blitzy (mainframe integration), Crop Diagnostix (agricultural fungal detection).
4. **Physical AI.** Intramotev (autonomous rail), AeroVect (airfield cargo), Agtonomy (farm management). Expects "two or three competitors instead of twenty or thirty" because of regulatory barriers.

## Relevance to Downstream

**Supports the thesis, from the investor side.** "Build on existing models" is the downstream position stated as an allocation strategy. Worth citing in post 1 as evidence that the opening is recognized by people who fund it, not only by people who live in it.

**"Unpromptable markets" is the skipped step by another name.** His line, "You can't get there by asking it a better question," is the whole argument: value in a vertical comes from the specific work the model can't do from a prompt. Direct support for posts 1 and 2. Borrow the term with attribution.

**Agent identity and permissions as an investment category** (Arcade) confirms that the gate problem in post 3 is a market, not a Dashbud idiosyncrasy. Potential example of a non-Dashbud garden-and-gate.

**Deterministic models as a category** rhymes with "same question, same answer" in post 4, though he means deterministic *models* and we mean a deterministic *artifact* authored by a probabilistic model. Different mechanisms, same customer need. Worth a sentence distinguishing them.

**Where we'd push back, gently.** The enterprise and small-business gaps are framed as adoption lag: the capability exists, people haven't caught up. The Downstream series says the lag is structural: nobody has taken the skipped step for those businesses yet, so there is nothing safe to adopt. Michelle at Trillium Creek is the counterexample to "almost none run daily operations on it": she does, personally, and the organization doesn't own it. That's a friendly disagreement and could be a response post or a comment on his piece.

**Anthropic S-1 reference** ("what a company looks like when it runs on its own product") is useful for the credit-upstream paragraph: the lab as the best example of a downstream user of its own model.
